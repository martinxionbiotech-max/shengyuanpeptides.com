Batch traceability is the difference between a supplier that hands you documentation and a supplier that can prove the documentation belongs to the vial in your hand. It connects the physical material to its records: the synthesis or sourcing batch, the sample that was tested, the certificate that was issued, the label that was applied, and the shipment that arrived.

A batch number printed on a label is a starting point, not a system. Traceability exists only when the supplier can walk the chain in both directions — from the delivered vial back to the test data, and from the test data forward to the shipped batch.

This guide explains how the chain is built, what breaks it, and how a buyer can check it before and after an order. Peptides are laboratory research materials; the traceability requirements that matter for a research reagent differ from those for raw-material procurement or regulated applications, and the difference determines how deep your checks should go. The broader framework is in our [supplier evaluation guide](/knowledge-base/how-to-evaluate-a-peptide-supplier/).

## What a Complete Traceability Chain Connects

A traceability chain links four kinds of records:

| Link | What it contains | What it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Production or sourcing record | Origin, source batch, or synthesis batch | Where did the material come from? |
| Quality control records | Sample identifiers, test results, review and disposition | What was tested, and what was the outcome? |
| Packaging and labeling | Lot identifiers, quantities, packaging records | Which vials carry this batch number? |
| Shipment and receiving | Order reference, shipped lot, delivery documentation | Which shipment reached which customer? |

The chain works when every link carries an identifier that also appears on the next link. The batch number connects the production record to the sample. The sample identifier connects the sample to the analytical report. The lot identifier connects the report to the label. The order reference connects the label to the shipment.

When a supplier buys finished material from another manufacturer, the chain begins with the original manufacturer's batch rather than an in-house production record. That is not a defect — the requirement is an unambiguous connection between source material, quality evidence, and delivered goods, whoever holds the records.

## The Identifiers That Carry the Chain

Four identifiers do most of the work in peptide traceability:

1. **Batch or lot number** — the unique identifier assigned to a production or sourcing batch. It should appear on the CoA, the label, and the shipment records.
2. **Sample identifier** — the code that ties the tested sample to the batch it was drawn from. Without it, a test result cannot be connected to anything physical.
3. **Report number** — the reference on the Certificate of Analysis or laboratory report, allowing the document itself to be verified.
4. **Order and shipment reference** — the commercial identifiers that connect a specific delivery to a specific buyer.

Ask for all four on the first order, not just the batch number. A supplier that can only produce a batch number may have a label, not a traceability system.

## How to Check the Chain Before an Order

Before committing, verify that the records the supplier shows you actually connect:

- [ ] Does the CoA carry the same batch number as the label you will receive?
- [ ] Is the tested sample identified, and does it belong to that batch?
- [ ] Are the test date, method, and results stated on the CoA?
- [ ] Does the label identify the product the same way the CoA does?
- [ ] Are packaging quantities consistent with the order?
- [ ] Can the supplier name the manufacturing or sourcing site for the batch?

A useful test is to ask for a specific historical batch — a past production run — and see how long it takes the supplier to retrieve its records. A supplier that can pull a complete batch file for a previous order has a working system; one that replies with a generic certificate does not.

## What Breaks the Chain

The most common breaks in peptide supply chains:

- **Repackaging without re-identification.** Material split into new vials without carrying the original batch identifier loses its connection to the original test data.
- **Relabeling under a new brand.** Private-label repacking is legitimate when documented; undocumented relabeling severs the chain.
- **Blending batches.** Combining two production batches into one lot without recording the composition destroys the link between test results and delivered material.
- **Generic CoAs.** A certificate that describes a product category rather than a specific batch cannot be traced to anything.
- **Missing sample records.** Test results without a sample identifier are floating evidence.

Each break has a documented, legitimate version. The question to ask the supplier is not "did this happen" but "where is the record of it happening."

## How to Check the Chain After Delivery

On receipt, complete the loop with the physical material:

1. Match the batch number on the label to the batch number on the CoA.
2. Match the product name, CAS, and quantity against the order.
3. Record the receiving date, storage conditions, and who accepted the material.
4. File the label copy with the CoA so the connection survives turnover in your own lab.
5. Log the batch number in your own records — your traceability system is the receiving end of the supplier's.

This five-minute routine is what makes a complaint or discrepancy investigation possible later. If a quality issue arises, the buyer who kept the receiving record can say exactly which batch, which shipment, and which documents are involved. The buyer who did not is reconstructing the chain from emails.

## Traceability and Repeat Orders

On repeat orders, ask about change control between batches: has the manufacturing site, raw material source, analytical method, or specification changed since the last order? A batch that tests identically but comes from a different site is a different product for traceability purposes, and the supplier should be able to say so.

Also confirm how long records are retained, and what happens to your traceability trail if the supplier changes its own sourcing. Some buyers write record-retention expectations into the contract; at minimum, the supplier should state its retention period in writing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between a batch number and a lot number?**
The terms are used interchangeably in most peptide supply chains. Both identify a defined quantity of material produced or handled under uniform conditions.

**Can I trust a supplier that repackages under its own label?**
Repackaging is legitimate when it is documented: the original batch identifier is retained and linked to the new label, and the repackaging record is available. Undocumented relabeling breaks traceability.

**How long should batch records be retained?**
There is no universal rule for research materials; regulated applications set their own requirements. Ask the supplier to state its retention period, and consider whether it matches how long you will need the records for your own projects.

**What should I do if the batch number on the label does not match the CoA?**
Stop and investigate before using the material. A mismatch means the documentation no longer describes the delivered goods. Ask the supplier to reconcile the discrepancy or provide the correct batch records.

**Does an online COA lookup improve traceability?**
It can. A lookup that returns the release data for the exact verification code on the label closes the loop between label and test record in one step — for example, our [COA lookup](/coa/) returns the batch's purity, peptide content, and identity from the code on the label, as used for products like [Tirzepatide](/products/tirzepatide/). The lookup is a convenience on top of the underlying batch records, not a substitute for them.

## Conclusion

Traceability is what turns a folder of certificates into evidence about the material you actually received. The identifiers are simple — batch, sample, report, shipment — and the checkpoints are simple too: before the order, after delivery, and between repeat orders.

A supplier that treats the chain as routine is also the supplier that can investigate a problem when one appears.
