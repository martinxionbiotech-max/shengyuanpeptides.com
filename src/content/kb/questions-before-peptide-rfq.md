# Questions to Ask a Peptide Supplier Before an RFQ

**Direct answer.** Send twelve questions before you send a specification. They cost one email, they force disclosure on capability rather than price, and the pattern of answers tells you more than any quotation will — because a supplier who cannot answer them will not be able to answer them later either, when the batch is late.

Part of the [peptide supplier evaluation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-evaluation/).

## Why before the RFQ, not after

Once a price is on the table, the conversation becomes commercial. Questions asked after the quote read as negotiation tactics; questions asked before it read as qualification, and suppliers answer them differently.

There is also a practical reason: an RFQ sent to a supplier who cannot make the material produces a quote anyway. The quote is real, the capability is not, and the difference surfaces at the sample stage — after you have spent two weeks.

## The twelve questions

Grouped by what they establish. Ask all twelve; a supplier who answers nine is still informative.

### Identity and capability

1. **Which legal entity would invoice me, and what is its registration number?**
   Lets you check the entity independently. A supplier who invoices from a different entity than the one you evaluated is telling you something.

2. **Do you synthesise in-house? If so, what is your largest reactor volume and how many lines do you run?**
   The single fastest test of manufacturer versus reseller.

3. **What purification capacity do you have for a sequence of this length, at the quantity I am considering?**
   Purification, not synthesis, is the usual bottleneck. Vague answers here predict delays.

4. **Do you lyophilise in-house or outsource it?**
   Outsourced lyophilisation adds a handoff where batch identity is easiest to lose.

### Quality and documentation

5. **Can you send a sample COA for a batch you shipped in the last three months — not a template?**
   A template tells you nothing. A recent batch tells you what their documents actually look like.

6. **Does the certificate state peptide content, or only purity?**
   Content determines what you weigh. A supplier who does not measure it cannot support quantitative work.

7. **Which laboratory performs your release testing, and is it accredited for that method?**
   "Our own lab" is an acceptable answer if they can describe the method; it is not acceptable as a complete answer.

8. **How long do you retain samples, and will you re-test one if I dispute a result?**
   The question that separates a manufacturer from a trader, and the one buyers forget to ask.

### Commercial and process

9. **What is your MOQ for this sequence, and does it change if the specification tightens?**
   MOQ is not a fixed property; it moves with purification difficulty.

10. **What is the lead time for a sample versus a bulk run, and what usually causes slippage?**
    The second half of the question is the useful part. Suppliers who answer it honestly describe their own constraints.

11. **Which Incoterms do you quote, and what is the validity period of the price?**
    Peptide prices move with resin and solvent costs. A quote with no validity is not a price.

12. **If the batch fails release, what happens — re-work, replacement, or credit?**
    Ask before you need to know. The answer is a preview of the dispute you may have.

## What strong and weak answers look like

| Question | Strong answer | Weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| Reactor volume | "50 L and 20 L, two lines" | "We have a large production base" |
| Purification | "C18, 50 mm ID, 8 g crude load per run" | "Advanced purification equipment" |
| Recent COA | A dated certificate with batch number and method | A blank template PDF |
| Retained samples | "Five years, re-test on request" | "We guarantee quality" |
| Failed batch | "Re-purify once, then credit" | "This never happens" |

The weak answers share a structure: they restate the promise instead of describing the process.

## How to read a partial answer

Not every question needs a perfect answer. What matters is whether the gaps are **structural** or **administrative**:

- *Structural gap*: they cannot describe purification capacity because they do not purify. That is a fact about what you are buying.
- *Administrative gap*: they do have the data but have not compiled it. That is a delay, and a supplier who says "give me two days" and then delivers is more credible than one who answers instantly with generalities.

A supplier who answers eight of twelve precisely, and explains the other four, is usually a better bet than one who answers all twelve vaguely.

## Our position

**Twelve questions is deliberately more than a buyer needs, because the pattern matters more than any single answer.** A supplier who answers the capability questions precisely and the documentation questions vaguely is a factory with weak paperwork — a manageable situation. The reverse is a reseller with borrowed documents, which is not.

**"This never happens" is the worst answer in the list.** Every manufacturer has had a batch fail release. A supplier who claims otherwise either has not been in the business long or is managing you rather than informing you.

**The questions should be sent as a document, not scattered across emails.** A written list produces written answers, which are comparable across suppliers and quotable in an internal approval. Scattered questions produce chat.

## A note on tone

These questions are direct, and some suppliers will read them as distrust. That is a cost worth paying: a supplier who responds defensively to a capability question is signalling that the question is unwelcome, which is itself informative. The ones who answer in detail tend to be the ones who have the details.
