# Supplier Qualification Documentation

**Direct answer.** Qualification documentation is about the *company*, not the product: who the legal entity is, what it is licensed to do, which certifications it holds and within what scope, and what it has agreed to in writing. Analytical records — COA, methods, batch data — belong to the [quality documentation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-quality-documentation/); confusing the two is how buyers end up with a folder of certificates and no supplier qualification.

Part of the [peptide supplier evaluation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-evaluation/).

## The document set

| Document | What it proves | What to check | Red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Business licence** | The entity exists and may trade | Legal name matches the invoice, registration number, business scope | Name differs from the website brand with no explanation |
| **Export licence / registration** | The entity may export the goods | Scope covers the product category; validity date | Expired, or covers a different category |
| **ISO 9001 certificate** | A QMS is in place ([ISO 9001:2015](https://www.iso.org/standard/62085.html)) | Scope statement covers manufacturing, not just trading; certification body is accredited | Scope says "trading of chemical products" |
| **ISO/IEC 17025 (if a lab)** | Testing competence within a scope ([ISO/IEC 17025:2017](https://www.iso.org/standard/66912.html)) | The specific method is inside the accredited scope | Certificate shown, scope never stated |
| **GMP certificate (if claimed)** | Manufacturing controls for API | Which authority, which product range | "GMP compliant" with no certificate |
| **Quality agreement** | What the supplier commits to on change, deviation, recall | Signed, dated, with a named contact | Template offered, never signed |
| **Insurance / product liability** | Financial backing if something goes wrong | Coverage amount and territory | Absent, for a supplier shipping internationally |
| **Audit report** | An independent assessment of the site | Auditor, date, findings, closure of findings | Report older than three years, or findings unresolved |

Eight documents. A typical supplier pack contains three, and one of them is usually a certificate whose scope contradicts the claim being made.

## The scope problem

Certification scope is where most buyer checks stop too early. A certificate is a claim about a *scope*, and the scope is printed on the certificate itself:

- ISO 9001 certified for "trading of chemical products" says nothing about manufacturing.
- ISO/IEC 17025 accredited for "pH and water content" says nothing about peptide purity by RP-HPLC.
- A GMP certificate for one product range says nothing about another.

The check takes thirty seconds: read the scope line, and compare it to what the supplier told you they do. Mismatches are common and are not always dishonest — companies genuinely hold certificates for parts of their business. The problem is when the buyer assumes the scope covers the purchase.

## Entity alignment

Three names should match: the website brand, the legal entity on the business licence, and the entity on the invoice and the COA.

| Mismatch | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Brand ≠ licence name | Normal — brands are traded names. Confirm the link in writing. |
| Licence name ≠ invoice name | The invoicing entity is different from the one you qualified. Ask why. |
| COA issuer ≠ invoice entity | You are buying a resold batch. See [manufacturer vs distributor](/knowledge-base/manufacturer-vs-distributor-peptides/). |
| Everything different | You may be dealing with an intermediary who has not disclosed the manufacturer. |

None of these is automatically disqualifying. All of them change who you have recourse against.

## The quality agreement

The document buyers most often skip, and the one that matters when something goes wrong. A workable quality agreement for research-grade peptide supply covers:

1. **Change notification** — what changes must be disclosed before shipment, and how far in advance.
2. **Deviation handling** — what happens when a batch fails the specification: re-work, replacement, credit, and who decides.
3. **Retention** — how long samples and records are kept, and the buyer's right to request re-testing.
4. **Complaint route** — a named contact, a response time, and an escalation path.
5. **Confidentiality** — sequence and specification confidentiality, both ways.

Five clauses. Without them, the relationship runs on email goodwill, which works until it does not.

## Our position

**A certificate without its scope is a logo, not a qualification.** The scope line is the only part of the document that distinguishes a relevant certification from an irrelevant one, and it is the part buyers skip. Reading it is the highest-value thirty seconds in supplier onboarding.

**Entity alignment catches more problems than any other single check.** If the licence, the invoice and the COA name three different companies, something in the chain is undisclosed. That is not necessarily fraud — but it means you cannot qualify the supplier you are actually buying from.

**The quality agreement should be signed before the first order, not after the first dispute.** It is cheap to agree when everyone is optimistic and expensive to negotiate when a batch has failed and money is held.

**Insurance is the document nobody asks for and everybody wishes they had.** For a supplier shipping internationally, product liability cover is a signal about how seriously they take exposure — and its absence is a signal too.

## A practical sequence

1. Request the business licence and export registration. Check the names against the invoice.
2. Request certificates **with scope pages**. Compare scope to claimed activity.
3. Ask for a sample quality agreement. If they have none, that is information.
4. Ask who signs release, and what their role is.
5. File all of it with a review date — certificates expire, and expired certificates are the most common audit finding.
