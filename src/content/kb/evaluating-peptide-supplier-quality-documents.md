# How to Evaluate a Peptide Supplier's Quality Documents

**Direct answer.** Score the documents, do not read them impressionistically. Five dimensions — identity evidence, purity evidence, content, traceability, and laboratory independence — each scored 0–3, give a 15-point picture that separates suppliers who manufacture from suppliers who resell, and it takes an afternoon rather than an audit.

Part of the [peptide quality documentation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-quality-documentation/).

## Why a score rather than a judgement

Most buyers evaluate documentation by reading the certificate and forming an impression: it looks thorough, the numbers are high, the layout is professional. That impression is strongly correlated with design budget, not with analytical rigour.

A score forces the two questions that matter: *what evidence exists*, and *can it be checked independently*. It also produces a number you can put in a sourcing memo, which is what a lab head or QA reviewer actually needs.

## The five dimensions

### 1. Identity evidence (0–3)

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Sequence absent, or "identity confirmed" with no mass |
| 1 | Theoretical mass stated, no measured value |
| 2 | Measured mass with method, compared to theoretical |
| 3 | LC-MS trace linking the measured mass to the main chromatographic peak |

### 2. Purity evidence (0–3)

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | "≥98% (HPLC)" and nothing else |
| 1 | Wavelength stated, nothing about column or gradient |
| 2 | Column, gradient, wavelength, integration threshold |
| 3 | As above, plus impurity profile by species and a chromatogram |

### 3. Peptide content (0–3)

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Not reported |
| 1 | "Content: conforms" without a value |
| 2 | Net peptide content reported, method named |
| 3 | Content plus water content and counter-ion, so the mass balance closes |

### 4. Traceability (0–3)

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | No batch number on the certificate |
| 1 | Batch number on the certificate only |
| 2 | Batch number on certificate and label; manufacture, test and release dates consistent |
| 3 | As above, plus retention samples and a stated retention period |

### 5. Laboratory independence (0–3)

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | No laboratory named |
| 1 | In-house laboratory named |
| 2 | Third-party laboratory named, selected by the supplier |
| 3 | Third-party laboratory named and selectable by the buyer, with the accredited scope stated |

## How to run it

1. **Request a real certificate for a real batch** — not a sample PDF from the website. Ask for the batch that would ship if you ordered today.
2. **Score each dimension against the table**, without adjusting for how convincing the supplier's email was.
3. **Ask two follow-up questions**: which laboratory ran the purity method, and what is the retention period for samples. The answers usually move the score down, not up.
4. **Total out of 15.** Below 6 is a datasheet supplier; 6–9 is typical of the market; 10–12 is a serious research supplier; 13+ is approaching pharmaceutical-grade documentation, which most research suppliers do not have.

## Reading the result

The score is less useful as an absolute than as a comparison. Two suppliers scoring 7 and 11 are not "both acceptable" — the second has evidence for dimensions the first cannot answer, and that difference predicts how each will behave when a batch goes wrong.

A low score is also not automatically disqualifying. If you are buying a peptide for a pilot experiment and the material is cheap, a 6/15 supplier may be entirely rational. The score becomes decisive when the material feeds a study you will have to defend, or when a failed experiment costs more than the peptide.

## The patterns behind the scores

**Resellers cluster at 4–6.** They can produce a certificate, because the manufacturer gave them one. They cannot produce a batch record, a retention sample or a method, because those live with the manufacturer. The tell is not the certificate — it is the inability to answer questions that require having made the material.

**Manufacturers cluster at 8–12.** They know their process and can describe it, but research-grade operations often skip content measurement and rarely publish impurity profiles by species.

**The 13+ suppliers are rare** and usually serve pharmaceutical customers as well. If a research-only supplier claims 15, ask for the accredited laboratory scope — the claim tends to soften at that point.

## Our position

**Documentation quality predicts manufacturing quality, which is why it is the cheapest audit available.** A supplier who cannot state the gradient on their own certificate is unlikely to control the gradient in production. You can learn this before spending money, which makes it the highest-return question in the entire purchasing process.

**The score should be reported as a range, not a verdict.** A 9/15 supplier with a clear explanation of what they do not measure is more trustworthy than a 13/15 supplier whose answers do not survive a follow-up. The number starts the conversation; consistency finishes it.

**Never score a supplier on material you have not seen tested.** The certificate describes a batch; your sample is the batch. The score tells you how much analytical work you still need to do yourself.

## Limits

This rubric assesses documentation, not the material. A well-documented batch can still fail your assay, and a poorly documented one can pass it. The score tells you how much of the verification burden remains on you — it does not remove it.
