# Peptide Supplier Evaluation and B2B Procurement

**Direct answer.** Most supplier evaluations fail before the first sample arrives, because the buyer evaluates a *website* and then negotiates a *price* — and capability is discovered only when a batch fails. The correction is procedural: establish who the seller actually is, force disclosure at the RFQ stage, then normalise quotations before comparing them. Everything in this pillar sits before the money moves, which is the only point where a buyer has leverage.

The companion pillar, [peptide quality documentation](/knowledge-base/peptide-quality-documentation/), covers what to do once documents exist. This one covers how to get them, and how to read the commercial signals around them.

## Why procurement in this market is structurally asymmetric

Three features make peptide buying harder than buying most laboratory reagents.

**The seller knows more than the catalogue shows.** A website can describe a catalogue of two hundred peptides while the company owns no synthesis line. Nothing on the page contradicts it.

**The buyer cannot inspect the goods.** A lyophilised powder looks like every other lyophilised powder. Verification requires analytical chemistry the buyer may not have, which pushes the burden onto documents and on the seller's willingness to be checked.

**Price anchors early and hard.** The first quote sets the reference point for every later conversation. A buyer who compares prices before comparing scopes will systematically select the supplier who scoped least.

The response to all three is the same: **make the seller disclose before you commit**, and compare like with like.

## The test that separates a manufacturer from a reseller

Three questions, all answerable in a single email. A reseller can bluff the first two; the third is where the bluff collapses.

1. **"What is the volume of your largest reactor, and how many synthesis lines do you run?"** A manufacturer answers with numbers specific to their plant. A reseller offers to "check with the factory".
2. **"Which purification system is used for this sequence, and what column dimensions?"** Purification capacity is the binding constraint in peptide production. Someone who makes the material knows whether their column can handle the crude load for a 40-mer at the quantity quoted.
3. **"Can you produce a batch record for a lot you shipped last quarter — redacted if necessary?"** A reseller has a certificate from the manufacturer, not a batch record. This is the question that separates them, because a batch record requires having made the batch.

If all three answers are vague, treat the seller as a trading company and price accordingly. That is not automatically disqualifying — traders can be efficient — but it changes what you are buying and what recourse you have.

## The evaluation sequence

Ordered by cost: each step costs more than the one before, so a failure early saves the expense of the rest.

| Step | What you are establishing | Cost to you |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Business identity | Legal entity, registration, export licence, years operating | Minutes |
| 2. Capability claims | Reactor scale, purification, lyophilisation, QC equipment | One email |
| 3. Documentation set | COA structure, specification, method summaries | One email |
| 4. Commercial terms | MOQ, lead time, Incoterms, payment, validity | RFQ |
| 5. Sample | Whether the material matches the documents | Sample cost + your assay |
| 6. Qualification audit | Whether the process is controlled | Days |

Most buyers start at step 4 and work backwards when something goes wrong. The sequence is reversed precisely because identity and capability are cheap to check and expensive to discover late.

## Normalising quotations

Two quotes for "1 gram of peptide X" are not comparable until five variables are aligned:

| Variable | Why it changes the number |
|---|---|
| Incoterms | FOB, CIF and DDP allocate freight, insurance and duty differently ([Incoterms 2020](https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/)) |
| Content basis | A price per gram of *powder* and a price per gram of *peptide* differ by 10–25% |
| Purity basis | 95% and 98% are different products with different purification cost |
| Testing scope | In-house COA versus third-party report is a cost line, not a free extra |
| Payment terms | 30% deposit versus 100% prepayment is a financing cost, and a risk allocation |

A quote that omits any of these is not a price; it is an opening position. The RFQ template in this pillar asks for all five so the comparison happens once, on paper, instead of three times, in email.

## The MOQ, lead time and specification triangle

Buyers routinely ask for low MOQ, short lead time and tight specification simultaneously. In peptide synthesis, you can usually have two.

- **Low MOQ + tight spec** means the purification burden is spread over a small batch: the per-gram price rises sharply.
- **Low MOQ + short lead time** means the sequence is run on a shared schedule, with less freedom to re-purify if the first pass underperforms.
- **Tight spec + short lead time** requires the process to be well controlled already, which is a property of the manufacturer, not of the order.

Understanding which corner you are buying is the difference between a realistic RFQ and a negotiation that ends in a substituted specification.

## What each document actually proves

Procurement teams frequently treat these as interchangeable. They are not.

| Claim | What it certifies | What it does not |
|---|---|---|
| Business licence | The entity exists and may trade | Anything about capability |
| ISO 9001 | A quality management system is in place ([ISO 9001:2015](https://www.iso.org/standard/62085.html)) | That any analytical method is fit for purpose |
| ISO/IEC 17025 | A laboratory is competent **within a stated scope** ([ISO/IEC 17025:2017](https://www.iso.org/standard/66912.html)) | Competence outside that scope, or that the sample was representative |
| GMP (ICH Q7) | Manufacturing controls for API production ([ICH Q7](https://www.ich.org/page/quality-guidelines)) | That a research-grade supplier operates under it |
| COA | Results for one batch | That the method behind them was valid |

The pattern: each document certifies a *system*, and buyers read them as if they certified a *result*.

## Our position

**The RFQ is the cheapest audit available, and most buyers waste it.** A well-designed RFQ — capability questions, normalised commercial fields, a request for a real batch record — extracts more information than a site visit, and it costs an email. Buyers who send "please quote 1 g of X" get a number and nothing else.

**"Manufacturer" is a claim, not a category.** The market is full of trading companies presenting factory photographs. The three-question test above is not foolproof, but it is cheap, and refusing to answer is itself an answer.

**Price comparison before scope normalisation is the most common procurement error in this category.** It reliably rewards the supplier who tests least. If two quotes differ by 40%, the first question is not "why are you expensive" but "what did the other quote exclude".

**Qualification documents should be requested before the sample, not after.** A supplier who cannot produce a business licence and an export record promptly is unlikely to produce a batch record promptly when a batch is disputed.

## Limits

This pillar describes commercial due diligence for research-grade peptide procurement. It does not cover regulatory qualification for pharmaceutical supply, which requires a supplier audit against GMP and a quality agreement — a different process with a different evidence standard.

Peptides discussed on this site are research materials, not for human or veterinary use.
