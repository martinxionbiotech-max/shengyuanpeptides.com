A certificate of analysis is only useful if it belongs to the batch in front of you. A generic catalogue certificate, a certificate with no batch number, or a report that lists results without methods cannot support a release decision.

This guide walks through the fields a buyer should check on a batch-specific COA. It complements the [peptide quality, COA and batch traceability guide](/quality/) and the [COA page](/coa/).

## What “Batch-Specific” Means

Batch-specific means the certificate is tied to a defined manufacturing or filling batch — not to a product name in general.

The link should be visible:

- batch or lot number on the certificate;
- the same number on the vial label or packing list;
- a manufacture date and, where applicable, a retest date;
- a release decision for that batch;
- a specification the results are measured against.

If the certificate could apply to any batch of the same product, it is not a batch record. It is a product description.

## Identity Fields

Start with the question: is this the material we ordered?

| Field | What to check |
|---|---|
| Product name | Matches the purchase order and label |
| Sequence or identifier | Present for custom or sequence-defined peptides |
| CAS number | Where applicable; not a substitute for sequence identity |
| Molecular weight | Consistent with the expected sequence and modifications |
| Salt form | Acetate, TFA or other agreed form |
| Batch / lot | Matches the vial and packing list |

A mass result should include the observed value and the expected value, not only the phrase “MS confirmed”. For modified peptides, the expected mass must include the modification.

## Method Fields

A result without a method is difficult to evaluate. The certificate should identify:

- the identity method (for example ESI-MS or MALDI-TOF);
- the HPLC column and dimensions;
- mobile-phase and gradient conditions, at least in summary;
- detection wavelength;
- integration basis;
- any additional method used for a specific test.

The method does not need to be a full laboratory SOP, but it should let a technical reviewer understand what was measured. “HPLC 99%” without column, wavelength or integration detail is not enough to compare two suppliers.

## Purity and Peptide Content

These are separate fields.

**Purity** is usually an area percentage from a chromatographic method. It describes the proportion of detected UV-absorbing material represented by the target peak. It is method-dependent.

**Peptide content** describes how much of the powder is peptide, after accounting for water and counterions. It is not the same as chromatographic purity.

A complete certificate should report both where the specification requires them. If only purity is shown, the buyer cannot calculate the peptide mass in the vial.

## Water Content and Counterions

Lyophilised peptide is not 100% dry peptide. Water and counterions are part of the product.

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Water content (Karl Fischer) | Affects weighing, stability and net peptide calculation |
| Counterion / salt form | Changes mass, pH behaviour and handling |
| Residual solvent | Relevant where the process or specification sets a limit |
| Net peptide content | Supports mass balance and research planning |

Do not assume the counterion from the product name. If the certificate says “acetate” or “TFA”, it should be consistent with the specification and the label.

## Release Tests

The release panel should match the specification and the intended research use.

Common fields include:

- appearance;
- identity;
- purity;
- peptide content;
- water content;
- pH where relevant;
- endotoxin where a limit is specified;
- residual solvents where required.

A certificate should not imply a test was performed if the method and result are absent. “Endotoxin controlled” without a value, method and limit is a claim, not release data.

## Batch Traceability

The certificate should connect to records that show how the batch was made and released.

Ask whether the supplier can provide:

- the batch record or a summary;
- the specification and acceptance criteria;
- raw HPLC and mass-spectrometry data;
- deviation or rework records, if any;
- storage and retest conditions;
- a change-control record if the method or process changed.

The certificate is the summary; the batch record is the evidence trail.

## Independent Testing Records

An independent laboratory report can add confidence, but only if the sample is traceable.

Check:

- who collected the sample;
- how it was identified and shipped;
- whether the report names the batch;
- which method the independent laboratory used;
- whether the result is a single sample or a batch release.

An independent result on an unidentified sample does not verify the batch you received.

## Red Flags

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| No batch number | Results cannot be tied to the material |
| Certificate reused across batches | No batch-specific release evidence |
| No method detail | Purity or identity cannot be evaluated |
| Purity reported as peptide content | Composition is unclear |
| No water or counterion data | Mass balance is incomplete |
| “Certified” without certificate scope | The claim cannot be verified |
| No release date or responsible function | The document is not a release record |

## A Practical Checklist

1. Match product, sequence and batch number to the order.
2. Confirm the manufacture and retest dates.
3. Read the identity method and observed result.
4. Read the HPLC method and purity result.
5. Check peptide content, water and counterion.
6. Check additional release tests against the specification.
7. Confirm the release decision and responsible function.
8. Request raw data or batch records where the risk justifies it.

If a field is missing, do not fill it in with an assumption. Ask for the record or treat the batch as incomplete for the intended use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a COA be batch-specific without a batch number?**

No. The batch identifier is the link between the document and the material. Without it, the certificate cannot be traced to the vial.

**Is HPLC purity the same as peptide content?**

No. Purity is a chromatographic area measure; peptide content describes the amount of peptide in the powder after water and counterions.

**Should every COA include endotoxin?**

Only where the specification or intended research use requires an endotoxin limit. If it is reported, the method, unit and limit should be clear.

**Can I accept a certificate without raw data?**

That depends on the risk and the supplier agreement. For critical batches, raw HPLC/MS data or a batch record summary may be appropriate.

**Does a certificate prove the supplier's quality system is certified?**

No. A batch certificate is a product record. Certification is a separate claim with its own scope and evidence.
