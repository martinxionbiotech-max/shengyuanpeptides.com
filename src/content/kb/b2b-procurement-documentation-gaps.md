# Common Documentation Gaps in B2B Peptide Procurement

**Direct answer.** Most supplier files look complete and fail at one of eight points: entity mismatch, expired certificates, scope drift, missing quality agreement, no change notification, undefined retention, no deviation procedure, or a batch number that never reaches the label. None of these is exotic; all of them surface after money has moved.

Part of the [peptide supplier evaluation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-evaluation/).

## The eight gaps

| # | Gap | How it appears | What it costs later |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Entity mismatch | Licence, invoice and COA name different companies | No clear counterparty to escalate to |
| 2 | Expired certification | Certificate valid, but the date has passed | Qualification is void on paper |
| 3 | Scope drift | Certified for trading, claimed as manufacturer | You qualified a different business than you bought from |
| 4 | No quality agreement | Everything agreed by email | Disputes resolved by goodwill |
| 5 | No change notification | Method or specification changes silently | Historical comparability lost |
| 6 | Undefined retention | No retained samples, no retention period | Cannot re-test a disputed batch |
| 7 | No deviation procedure | "This never happens" | Failure is handled ad hoc |
| 8 | Batch number not on the label | Number exists on the COA only | Traceability breaks at repackaging |

Eight. A typical file has three to five of them, and the buyer discovers the rest during the first problem.

## Gap 1 — entity mismatch

The most consequential and the easiest to check. Put three names side by side: the entity on the business licence, the entity on the invoice, the entity issuing the COA.

If the invoice comes from a Hong Kong trading company and the COA from a mainland manufacturer, you are buying through an intermediary. That may be deliberate and efficient — but your contract, your recourse and your audit rights all point at the trader, not the factory.

**Close it:** ask for the relationship in writing, and add the manufacturer as a named party in the quality agreement where possible.

## Gap 2 — expired certification

Certificates have dates. Files get reused. A three-year-old ISO 9001 certificate that expired last spring is still sitting in the supplier pack because nobody updated it.

**Close it:** record expiry dates in your supplier file and set a review reminder. This is administrative, and it is the most common finding in any audit of a buyer's own records.

## Gap 3 — scope drift

A supplier holds ISO 9001 for "trading of chemical products" and presents it as evidence of manufacturing capability. The certificate is genuine; the inference is not.

**Close it:** read the scope line. Compare it to the activity you are buying. If it does not match, ask what covers manufacturing.

## Gap 4 — no quality agreement

Without a signed agreement, change notification, deviation handling, retention and complaint routes exist only as email habits.

**Close it:** ask for their standard quality agreement. If none exists, propose a one-page version covering four items: change notification, deviation handling, retention, and a named complaint contact. A supplier who will not sign a one-page agreement has told you how disputes will go.

## Gap 5 — no change notification

A method changes, a subcontractor changes, a specification quietly loosens. Each batch is individually valid and collectively incomparable.

**Close it:** require notification before shipment for any change affecting specification, method, site or subcontractor. Put it in the agreement, not in an email.

## Gap 6 — undefined retention

Retained samples turn a dispute into a re-test. Without them, a dispute becomes an argument about who is responsible.

**Close it:** ask for the retention period and whether re-testing is available on request. "We keep samples for five years" is an answer; "we guarantee quality" is not.

## Gap 7 — no deviation procedure

Every manufacturer has batches that fail release. A supplier with a procedure describes it; one without says it does not happen.

**Close it:** ask what happens when a batch fails — re-work, replacement, credit — and who decides. Get it in writing before the first order.

## Gap 8 — batch number not on the label

The chain from certificate to vial runs through the label. When the label carries only a product name, repackaging breaks traceability without anyone noticing.

**Close it:** specify in the purchase order that the batch number appears on the primary label, and check it on arrival.

## A pre-signature checklist

Before the first purchase order:

- [ ] Business licence, export registration, and invoice entity all consistent
- [ ] Every certificate in date, with scope read and matched to activity
- [ ] Quality agreement signed, covering change, deviation, retention and complaints
- [ ] Retention period stated in writing
- [ ] Deviation procedure described in writing
- [ ] Batch number required on the primary label, in the PO terms
- [ ] Named technical contact, not only a sales address

Seven items. They take an afternoon and they cover the eight gaps.

## Our position

**These gaps are boring, which is why they persist.** None requires analytical chemistry or a site visit; all of them require someone to read a scope line and write a clause. The failure is procedural, and so is the fix.

**The cheapest single fix is requiring the batch number on the primary label.** It costs nothing, it is enforceable in the purchase order, and it preserves the one link that repackaging destroys. Buyers who do only one thing from this page should do that.

**An unsigned quality agreement is a gap even when the relationship is good.** Relationships change with staff, and the agreement is what survives the change.

**A supplier who resists these eight items is not necessarily hiding something.** Small suppliers often have no template for a quality agreement and no one who has been asked before. The useful question is not whether they have one, but whether they will produce one when asked.
