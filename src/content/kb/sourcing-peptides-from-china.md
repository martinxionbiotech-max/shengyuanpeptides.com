China hosts a deep ecosystem of peptide chemistry suppliers, and sourcing from it can deliver excellent quality at competitive cost. Distance, however, puts a premium on documentation: most buyers will never walk the production floor, so due diligence has to be done with paper, data, and pointed questions. This checklist covers what to verify before ordering and when a shipment arrives. Throughout, peptides are treated strictly as laboratory research materials — not for human or veterinary use — and no legitimate supplier should be asked or expected to provide dosing or medical guidance.

## Know Your Supplier Tier

Not all suppliers are equivalent, and the tier you need depends on your stage of research:

- GMP CDMO: a contract development and manufacturing organization operating under a formal pharmaceutical quality system — batch records, change control, qualified equipment, and investigations of out-of-specification results. Choose this tier when documentation trails or future clinical supply matter.
- ISO-certified laboratory: holds a recognized quality-management certification such as ISO 9001 and is well suited to routine research peptides.
- Trader or reseller: has no manufacturing capability of its own; it resells catalog items and adds convenience but limited technical depth and traceability.

Ask directly who manufactures the peptide. Many "manufacturers" are resellers with a website, and the difference shows up in the QC data.

## Verify the Paperwork: COA, HPLC, MS

Every batch should ship with a batch-specific certificate of analysis (COA) that includes the lot number, sequence, measured molecular weight, HPLC purity with method details, peptide content, counter-ion, residual water, and the date and signature of QC release. The COA should be accompanied by the actual HPLC chromatogram and mass spectrum, not just a table of numbers. Check that the observed mass matches theory and that the main peak is where it should be. For cell-based work, request an endotoxin report, typically LAL testing; it is usually not included unless asked for.

## Ask for Raw Data, Not Just Headline Numbers

A printed "95% purity" is a claim; an integrated chromatogram is evidence. Request the chromatogram with integration, the MS spectrum, and the method details — column, gradient, and UV wavelength. Suppliers that share raw analytical data are generally confident in it. For critical studies, commission an independent re-test of the first shipment on your own HPLC/MS. It is inexpensive insurance and establishes a baseline against which later batches can be compared.

## Verify Authenticity Codes and Batch Traceability

Some manufacturers print scratch-off verification codes on the COA or vial label that can be checked on their website. If present, verify them — they confirm the product came from the claimed source. Equally important is batch traceability: the lot number should connect to a synthesis record, raw-material lots, QC data, and a retained sample. Ask how long records and retention samples are kept, and record the lot number in your laboratory notebook so any later question can be traced.

## Audit the Quality System Behind the Batch

For routine research peptides, a documentation review may suffice: ask for quality certifications such as ISO 9001, including the certificate's scope and certifying body, and verify them. For larger programs or regulated-quality supply, ask how the supplier handles standard operating procedures, equipment calibration, staff training, and out-of-specification results. A remote or on-site audit is normal practice before committing to a major program; a supplier that resists scrutiny at this stage is telling you something important.

## Communication and Technical Support

The quality of a supplier's answers is diagnostic. Technical staff should be able to discuss synthesis strategy, explain a chromatogram, and advise on difficult sequences, not just quote a price. Communication in clear English — or your working language — matters, because a misunderstanding about a terminal modification travels badly across a supply chain. A trial order of a small or standard peptide is a low-cost way to test the entire chain: data quality, packaging, shipping, and paperwork, before committing to a larger program.

## Shipping, Cold Chain, and Customs

Most lyophilized peptides are shipped dry at ambient temperature for short transits, with cold packs and insulated packaging used for longer routes; long or cysteine-rich peptides benefit from cold chain. On arrival, store according to the COA, commonly -20°C for lyophilized peptides. For customs, the supplier should declare the goods accurately as laboratory research reagents or chemicals, explicitly "not for human or veterinary use," and provide an MSDS and COA for clearance. Import duties, Incoterms, and any import permits are the buyer's responsibility, so confirm your country's rules before ordering. Inspect packaging on arrival and re-quarantine any shipment that arrived warm when cold chain was specified.

## Red Flags to Avoid

- No batch-specific COA, or a COA that is clearly reused across orders.
- Refusal to share chromatograms or spectra.
- Prices dramatically below the market — ask what is being given up.
- No verifiable physical address or laboratory.
- Pressure to pay into personal accounts.
- "GMP" claims with no documentation to support them.
- Unwillingness to discuss QC data, audits, or out-of-specification handling.

Any one of these is a reason to pause; several together are a reason to walk away.
