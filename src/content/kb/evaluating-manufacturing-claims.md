# How to Evaluate Manufacturing Claims

**Direct answer.** Manufacturing claims split into three groups: those you can verify from documents (equipment lists, batch records), those you can test with a question only an operator could answer (column dimensions, crude load, cycle times), and those you cannot verify remotely at all (yield, consistency, contamination history). Spend your effort on the second group — it is where borrowed capability falls apart.

Part of the [peptide supplier evaluation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-evaluation/).

## The four capability claims that matter

Synthesis, purification, lyophilisation and analytical control. A supplier can be strong in one and weak in another, and the weak link sets the lead time.

### 1. Synthesis capacity

| Claim | The question that tests it | What a real answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| "Large-scale synthesis" | Largest reactor volume, number of lines | "50 L and 20 L, two lines, 3–5 kg crude per month" |
| "Custom synthesis" | Longest sequence made in the last year | A specific length and a specific difficulty |
| "High yield" | Typical crude purity for a 30-mer | A range, with the conditions that shift it |

Capacity is not the same as throughput. A 50 L reactor running difficult sequences produces far less than its volume suggests, and a supplier who quotes capacity without yield is quoting the vessel, not the output.

### 2. Purification capacity

This is the binding constraint, and the most revealing question.

| Claim | Question | Weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| "Preparative HPLC" | Column internal diameter and crude load per run | "Advanced purification system" |
| "High purity" | How many passes for 98% on this sequence | "Normally one" |
| "Scale-up available" | How many runs for 10 g at 98% | Silence, or a revised lead time |

Purification capacity explains most lead-time surprises. A supplier with a 20 mm column and a supplier with a 50 mm column can both make 100 mg; only one can make 10 g on schedule.

### 3. Lyophilisation

| Claim | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| "In-house lyophilisation" | Freeze-dryer shelf area and batch size | Outsourcing adds a handoff where batch identity is lost |
| "Consistent appearance" | Cycle time for a 2 g batch | Long cycles queue, and queues slip |

Outsourced lyophilisation is common and not disqualifying — but it means the supplier does not control the last physical step before shipment, and the batch record should say who does.

### 4. Analytical control

| Claim | Question | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| "In-house QC" | Which instruments: HPLC model, MS type | "We have a lab" with no instruments named |
| "Full testing" | Which tests run on every batch vs by request | A list that matches the certificate exactly, with no extras |
| "Third-party verification" | Which laboratory, and can I nominate one | "We use several" with no names |

## The borrowed-capability tells

Five patterns that suggest the claims belong to someone else:

1. **Photographs without metadata.** Factory images that appear on several suppliers' websites, or stock photography with no equipment detail.
2. **Numbers that are round and unspecific.** "Thousands of peptides", "large production base", "advanced equipment".
3. **Answers that redirect to the sales team.** "Our technical team will confirm" — and then never does.
4. **A certificate issued by a different company.** The manufacturing claims may be accurate; they are just not the seller's.
5. **Lead times that do not match the equipment described.** A 20 L reactor and a five-day turnaround for a custom 40-mer are inconsistent.

None of these is proof. Together they justify asking for a redacted batch record, which is where the question resolves.

## What cannot be verified remotely

Three things, and it is worth being explicit so you do not over-trust the rest:

- **Yield consistency.** A supplier can show one good batch; consistency is a distribution, not a data point.
- **Contamination history.** Cross-contamination between sequences is invisible in a COA unless you look for the specific carryover.
- **Staff competence.** Turnover, training and supervision do not appear in documents.

These are the reasons a site audit exists. If the purchase is large enough to justify one, the audit should focus here — not on re-reading certificates the supplier already sent.

## Our position

**Purification capacity is the question that separates factories from intermediaries, and almost nobody asks it.** It is technical enough that a salesperson cannot improvise an answer, and concrete enough that an operator answers in one sentence. If a buyer asks only one manufacturing question, it should be this one.

**A redacted batch record is worth more than a factory tour.** Tours show the building; the record shows the process for the lot you are buying. Suppliers who can produce one for a recent lot are almost always manufacturers, because the record requires having made the batch.

**"In-house" is a claim that should be attached to a specific step.** Synthesis, purification, lyophilisation and QC can each be in-house or outsourced, in any combination. Suppliers say "in-house manufacturing" when one of the four is, and buyers hear all four.

**Claims that cannot be tested should be discounted, not disbelieved.** A supplier may genuinely have a large plant. But if you cannot test it, it should not carry weight in the decision — the testable claims should.

## A compact script

If you want to ask five things and stop:

1. Largest reactor volume and line count.
2. Purification column diameter and crude load per run.
3. Lyophilisation in-house or outsourced.
4. Instruments used for release testing.
5. A redacted batch record for a recent lot.

Five questions, one email. The pattern of answers is the evaluation.
