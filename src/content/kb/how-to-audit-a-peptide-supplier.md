An audit answers the question that documentation alone cannot: does the supplier's paperwork describe how the supplier actually works? It is the step between reviewing a folder of certificates and trusting that folder with a production order.

Not every purchase needs an audit. The depth should match the risk: the material, the intended application, the regulatory obligations, the volume, and the consequences of failure. A noncritical research reagent may justify a document review and a remote conversation; a critical material for regulated manufacturing may justify an on-site audit with follow-up. Peptides are laboratory research materials, and audit depth should be proportionate — a research-use reagent does not require pharmaceutical-scale auditing, while raw-material procurement for regulated use may.

This guide covers the four stages of a practical supplier audit: document review, remote audit, on-site audit, and corrective action with requalification. It sits inside the larger framework of [supplier evaluation](/knowledge-base/how-to-evaluate-a-peptide-supplier/) and pairs with the [due diligence checklist](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-due-diligence-checklist/) you run before committing to an audit at all.

## Stage 1: Document Review — The Audit Before the Audit

A document review costs nothing but time and identifies the gaps a later audit should focus on. Request:

- Company and facility information — legal entity, operating address, and the relationship between them
- Quality management procedures and applicable certifications, with scopes
- Product specifications and representative batch records
- Analytical method information and sample certificates of analysis
- Deviation, complaint, and change-control procedures
- Outsourced manufacturing and testing arrangements, and how external providers are qualified
- Storage, shipping, and handling controls

Read these as evidence of a working system, not as a system itself. A procedure that exists only as a document is worth less than a shorter procedure the supplier can demonstrate against real records. Note every gap and use the list to structure the next stage.

## Stage 2: Remote Audit — Targeted Questions and Live Retrieval

A remote audit — a structured call or video session with the supplier's quality and technical staff — is sufficient for an initial assessment of lower-risk activities and is a necessary precursor to any site visit.

The most revealing technique in a remote audit is live record retrieval. Ask the supplier to pull a specific batch file while you are on the call: the batch record, the sample identifiers, the test results, the release decision. A supplier that can retrieve a complete batch file in minutes has a working record system; one that promises to send documents "later" and never does has exposed its documentation layer.

Cover the same ground as the document review, but as questions about practice:

- How are incoming raw materials identified and accepted?
- How are batch identifiers assigned, and where do they appear?
- Who reviews analytical results, and what triggers a batch rejection?
- How is an out-of-specification result investigated, and who closes the investigation?
- How are changes to materials, methods, or manufacturing sites controlled?
- How are complaints traced back to affected batches and customers?

Ask each question the same way across suppliers, and record the answers against the documents you reviewed in Stage 1. Inconsistencies between what the procedure says and what the staff describes are audit findings in themselves.

## Stage 3: On-Site Audit — Verifying the Physical Reality

An on-site audit is warranted when the supplier's facilities, production controls, or regulatory obligations require direct verification — and when the commercial stakes justify the cost. During the visit, look for evidence that procedures are implemented consistently, not merely stored in a document library.

Concrete checks that separate a working system from a folder of documents:

- **Batch retrieval.** Ask for a specific past batch and follow it from the record to the physical inventory, the label, and the shipment file.
- **Out-of-specification example.** Ask the quality staff to walk through their most recent OOS investigation — how it was triggered, investigated, and closed.
- **Equipment and maintenance.** Ask to see maintenance records for a critical instrument and the current calibration status.
- **Material status control.** Observe how quarantined, released, and rejected material is physically segregated and labeled.
- **Sample handling.** Watch how a sample is taken, labeled, and logged — the sample identifier is where traceability begins.
- **Clean-room behavior.** If controlled areas are claimed, observe how the claim matches the physical environment and the written procedures.

A site visit is also the place to verify identity claims made on paper: the facility matches the address, the equipment matches the capabilities described, and the people you meet are the people responsible for quality release.

## Stage 4: Findings, Corrective Actions, and Requalification

An audit produces findings, not a verdict. Classify findings by impact:

| Classification | Meaning | Typical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Evidence missing for a mandatory requirement; product or data integrity at risk | Immediate corrective action; order qualification suspended until closed |
| Major | A required control is not implemented or not effective | Corrective action with owner, deadline, and verification |
| Minor | An isolated gap or documentation weakness | Scheduled correction, tracked at the next review |

For each significant finding, assign a corrective action with a responsible person and a completion date. The key discipline is verification: do not close a finding because a response was submitted — close it when evidence shows the correction works. A supplier that treats corrective actions as paperwork will repeat the same finding next audit.

Set a requalification schedule proportionate to risk before the first order, and trigger an early review on significant change: a new manufacturing site, a new raw material source, a specification change, a pattern of complaints, or a regulatory change in the destination market.

## What an Audit Does Not Do

An audit is evidence about a point in time, not a guarantee about future batches. A clean audit means the controls were in place on the days examined; it does not predict the next production run. This is why the audit sits inside a continuous process — order-level verification, receiving checks, and periodic requalification — rather than standing alone as a one-time event.

The same discipline applies to the documents the supplier gives you. A batch-specific [CoA lookup](/coa/) verifies the material you received against the release data for that batch; it complements, and does not replace, your own receiving checks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to audit a supplier for a small research order?**
Rarely at site level. A document review and a structured remote call are proportionate for most research-scale purchases. Scale the audit to the material, the application, and the consequences of failure.

**How do I verify a remote audit is meaningful?**
Ask for live record retrieval during the call. The supplier's ability to pull a specific batch file on demand — rather than sending documents later — is the single most informative test.

**What should I do with audit findings?**
Classify by impact, assign corrective actions with owners and deadlines, and verify effectiveness before closing. Never close a finding on the strength of a submitted response alone.

**How often should I re-audit?**
Set a risk-based schedule before the first order and re-audit early on significant change: manufacturing site, raw material source, specification, complaint pattern, or regulatory change.

**Can a clean audit replace independent testing?**
No. An audit verifies that controls exist and operate; independent testing verifies analytical claims for a specific sample. They answer different questions. For testing verification, see [how to verify independent peptide testing records](/knowledge-base/how-to-verify-independent-peptide-testing-records/).

## Conclusion

A supplier audit is a verification exercise with a simple structure: review the documents, question the practice, verify the physical reality, and close the gaps with evidence. The supplier that performs well under audit is usually the supplier that performs well under order — and the audit trail you build is what makes future quality decisions defensible.
