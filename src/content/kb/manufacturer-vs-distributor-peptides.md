# Manufacturer vs Distributor: What Buyers Should Verify

**Direct answer.** A manufacturer can describe their own plant, produce a batch record for a lot they made, and re-purify a failed batch. A distributor can do none of those things but can often quote faster and cheaper on small quantities. Neither is wrong — but you cannot price, audit or escalate to them the same way, and the mistake is paying manufacturer assumptions against distributor reality.

Part of the [peptide supplier evaluation pillar](/knowledge-base/peptide-supplier-evaluation/).

## Why the label matters commercially

The distinction changes four things, all of them practical:

| | Manufacturer | Distributor |
|---|---|---|
| Price basis | Cost of goods plus margin | Someone else's price plus margin |
| Lead time | Bound by their production schedule | Bound by the factory's schedule, plus their own |
| Recourse on a failed batch | Re-test, re-purify, replace | Negotiate with the factory on your behalf |
| Documentation depth | Batch record, method, retained sample | Certificate supplied by the factory |
| Custom synthesis | Possible | Brokered, if at all |

A distributor quoting a shorter lead time than the factory is quoting optimism. A distributor quoting a lower price than the factory's own list usually means either a different specification or a different batch.

## The three questions

**1. "What is your largest reactor volume, and how many synthesis lines do you run?"**
Manufacturers answer in litres and line counts. Distributors say "we have partner factories" — which is an honest answer, and a complete answer to the question of what they are.

**2. "For this sequence at this quantity, what crude load does your purification column see, and what is the column diameter?"**
This is the question that exposes borrowed capability. Purification is the bottleneck in peptide production; someone who runs a column knows its dimensions and its load limit. Someone who resells has never seen it.

**3. "Can you send a redacted batch record for a lot you shipped last quarter?"**
A batch record shows the synthesis date, the resin and reagents, in-process checks and the release decision. A factory can produce one. A distributor can produce the certificate that came with it, and will usually say so.

## What the documents look like on each side

| Document | Manufacturer | Distributor |
|---|---|---|
| Business licence | Own entity, manufacturing scope | Own entity, trading scope |
| ISO 9001 | Own certificate, with scope | May hold one for logistics, not production |
| Export licence | Own | Own, covering resale |
| COA | Issued by them | Issued by the factory; letterhead differs |
| Method summary | Their own method | The factory's method, if released |
| Retained samples | Theirs | Usually none |
| Change notification | Theirs to give | Depends entirely on the factory |

The tell is in the letterhead: if the COA is issued by a company other than the one invoicing you, you are buying from a distributor — which is fine, as long as you know.

## The factory tour test

Site visits are useful but easy to stage: a clean room, a row of chromatography skids, staff in coats. Three things are harder to stage:

1. **Ask to see the batch record for the lot you are being quoted**, not a showpiece lot. Staging a record that matches your order is much harder than staging a facility.
2. **Ask who signs the release**, and what their qualification is. A name and a role are checkable.
3. **Ask what happens when a batch fails release.** A manufacturer describes a re-work or rejection process; a distributor describes an email to the factory.

## Why buyers get this wrong

**Because the market rewards the appearance of manufacturing.** Factory photographs, "our production base" pages and certifications are cheap to produce and impossible for a buyer to verify remotely. The rational response is not cynicism but procedure: ask the three questions, and treat vague answers as a data point rather than a rejection.

**Because distributors are genuinely useful.** For a small order of a catalogue peptide, a distributor with stock in a local warehouse can deliver in days, where a factory quotes three weeks. Paying a distributor margin for speed and convenience is a legitimate purchase.

**Because the cost of confusion is deferred.** The problem does not appear at purchase; it appears when a batch underperforms and the buyer discovers there is no retained sample, no method, and no one who made the material to ask.

## Our position

**"Manufacturer" should be a verified claim, not a marketing category.** The check costs one email. Buyers who skip it are not being careless so much as optimistic — and optimism is expensive when a study fails and there is no one to escalate to.

**A distributor is the right supplier for some purchases and the wrong one for others.** Stock items, small quantities, urgent replacement: distributor. Custom sequence, scale-up, a specification you need to defend: manufacturer. The error is using one for the other's job.

**The COA letterhead is the cheapest tell in the market.** If the certificate is issued by a company that is not the one invoicing you, you are buying a resold batch. That is not a scandal — it is information, and it should change your questions, not necessarily your decision.

## What to do with the answer

If the supplier is a distributor and that suits your purchase, ask two additional questions before ordering:

1. **Which factory made this batch, and can you confirm the batch number with them?**
2. **If the batch fails my assay, who re-tests the retained sample?**

If they cannot answer the second, the practical position is that you have no recourse beyond replacement from stock — which is worth knowing before you pay, not after.
